# Teaching Statement

My commitment to teaching began when I volunteered in under-resourced schools and saw how unequal access to education narrows students' opportunities. It was refined through my master's thesis on fostering creativity in higher education. I see education as a powerful equalizer, and how we teach shapes how students think. As a public health scholar studying health disparities, I recognize in health the same structural inequities I first saw in education, itself a determinant of health. This conviction anchors my philosophy: I teach to cultivate curious, critical thinkers prepared to advance health equity in the communities they will serve. My approach integrates evidence-based, inclusive practices that build a learning environment students can join without fear or judgment, grounded in pedagogical wellness, the holistic support of students' scholarly and emotional development [1].

At UC Irvine, I have put this philosophy into practice with more than 500 students across in-person, hybrid, and online asynchronous formats. I have served as instructor of record for lower- and upper-division public health courses, teaching assistant for six courses from honors seminars to large introductory classes, facilitator training 27 incoming graduate teaching assistants in the health sciences, and now Educational Development Scholar supporting new instructors. A year-long Pedagogical Scholar fellowship deepened this evidence-based practice. In my most recent course, 84% of students rated my overall teaching 9 out of 9, with metrics averaging 8.78 out of 9 across all dimensions. My doctoral training and quantitative expertise prepare me to teach across the graduate public health curriculum, from biostatistics, epidemiology, data analytics, and research methods to health disparities, environmental health, community health, and health policy.

## Creating Engaging Learning Environments Through Active Pedagogy

I build classroom communities where students feel safe taking intellectual risks while developing the critical thinking and scientific inquiry skills public health demands. I open each session with gamified, low-stakes retrieval practice, letting students apply new knowledge without the anxiety of high-stakes grading. In my Social Epidemiology course, Kahoot! review quizzes helped students build confidence: 53% identified them as effective, and one noted they "created a positive learning environment." I also structure courses around collaborative, case-based work, because building community in the classroom supports student success [2]. Using real public health data, students analyzed the immigrant health paradox and structural determinants in small groups. This pedagogy resonates: 82% found in-class practice helpful, 76% valued small group work, and students rated the environment 8.84 out of 9 for openness and fairness and 8.74 for encouraging critical thinking. One reflected that collaboration "helps maintain focus due to not only sitting and listening all the time but being able to express ideas."

Active learning also extends beyond the classroom. My community-based participatory research on cardiovascular health disparities taught me to position students as collaborators with communities rather than distant observers. I design learning that mirrors the community-engaged research process: students begin with community-identified concerns, weigh evidence through a health equity lens, and develop recommendations that honor community knowledge alongside empirical findings. Students examined social isolation among older adults and proposed evidence-based interventions. This teaches public health as a practice, not only a body of knowledge, and I am eager to deepen it through community-based learning partnerships connecting graduate students with local organizations.

## Teaching Quantitative Methods with Rigor and Support

Quantitative courses are where many students decide whether they belong in public health, so I teach them with rigor and encouragement. Eight years of statistical programming in SAS, R, Python, and Stata, my role as lead biostatistician in my current lab, and dissertation research applying epidemiological methods to cardiovascular outcomes among Asian Americans ground my teaching in real analytic practice. My work spans chronic and infectious disease, including co-first-authored research on community health engagement during respiratory infectious disease outbreaks. Case-based learning with real health datasets teaches study design, causal inference, and effect modification, while hands-on coding exercises build skills from descriptive statistics through mixed-effects models, survival analysis, and introductory machine learning. Disaggregated geographic analyses of stroke prevalence across California in ArcGIS let me bring spatial perspectives into data analytics instruction. Across these topics, I break complex methods into scaffolded steps, treat low-stakes mistakes as information rather than penalties, and anchor every technique in a substantive public health question. When students see that a regression model can reveal or conceal a disparity depending on its specification, methods become tools they want to master. I have coached students one-on-one in statistical modeling in SAS and R and watched anxious students grow into confident analysts.

## Responsive Teaching and Continuous Improvement

Effective teaching requires systematic assessment and adaptation. Drawing on my Pedagogical Scholar training, I center assessment for learning over assessment of learning. I administer pre-course surveys to understand students' backgrounds and barriers, enabling differentiated instruction, and midterm evaluations for timely feedback. When midterm responses called for earlier materials and clearer final-project instructions, I revised the guidelines, posted materials sooner, and added in-class work sessions with real-time feedback. When students noted fast pacing, I slowed transitions and built in consolidation pauses. The result: 74% rated my presentations and explanations 9 out of 9 in final evaluations, compared with mixed midterm responses. These practices extend across modalities and instructional teams. Teaching an online asynchronous course with 78 students, I coordinated a three-person instructional team through weekly meetings, collaboratively built detailed Canvas rubrics for consistent grading and feedback, and monitored progress closely. Because online formats can obscure struggling students, I proactively checked performance and held one-on-one Zoom meetings early. Designing assessments aligned with explicit learning outcomes prepares me to contribute to competency-based curricula and CEPH assessment requirements. I treat every course as a chance to listen, adjust, and teach better than the term before, pursuing continuing professional development through workshops, teaching communities, and pedagogical scholarship.

## Mentoring and Career Advising for Future Public Health Professionals

Inside and outside the classroom, I mentor students through academic and professional development. As teaching assistant for the Public Health Honors Seminar and Thesis across multiple quarters and now as a graduate mentor, I have guided students through every stage of a year-long research experience, from research design and methodology selection through data analysis and scholarly presentation. This closely parallels advising graduate students through applied practice and culminating experiences, and I am prepared to supervise graduate theses and projects. In my own courses, major assignments build research skills aligned with career goals: Social Epidemiology students choose a literature review, a research proposal, or a full data analysis as their final project. One wrote that the group research project "could be helpful in the future career when I need to create my own research project." This mentorship reshapes trajectories: several students entered public health graduate programs after working with me. One wrote that my course "had a profound impact" and "helped solidify my decision to pursue a graduate degree in public health," adding that our discussions "helped me discover an area I feel deeply passionate about." Another, from a non-public health background, said my course "shaped my academic and professional interests." My career advising draws on practice experience: nearly three years as a Data and Policy Analyst developing Medicare cost measures for the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services, service as Coordinator of the Applied Public Health Statistics Section of the American Public Health Association, and more than $90,000 in competitive funding, including an American Heart Association Fellowship. I use this background to coach students on grant development, from specific aims to budget justification, and on the full range of public health careers.

## Developing Fellow Educators

Teaching is my craft, and I take real joy in helping other instructors grow into it. Much of my recent teaching has served graduate students themselves. I co-designed and led a two-day professional development program for 27 incoming graduate teaching assistants, with workshops on learning outcomes, office hours, assessment, student communication, diversity and inclusion, and micro-teaching. I modeled evidence-based pedagogy through collaborative case studies and peer feedback, and 100% of participants felt prepared to teach and rated the environment as inclusive and respectful. I now co-lead the Summer Teaching Apprenticeship Program, which prepares 25 graduate students across eight academic units for their first terms as instructors of record in in-person, hybrid, and synchronous or asynchronous online formats. I provide individualized feedback on their syllabi and weekly assignments. Developing confident, reflective educators has sharpened my own teaching and prepared me to collaborate closely with instructional teams and strengthen academic programs.

I would welcome the chance to bring these commitments to graduate public health education, including new courses in advanced data analytics, artificial intelligence in health, and social determinants of health. My goal in every course is the same: students who leave more curious, more rigorous, and more committed to health equity.
