# Measuring Sleep at the Household and Family Level: A Narrative Review of Validated Instruments

## Overview

The existing literature offers a modest but usable set of validated, scored instruments for characterizing sleep at the level of the home environment and the family, rather than only the individual. These measures fall along two axes that matter for a family- or household-level study: *who provides the report* (the sleeping individual, a caregiver, or an objective/structural indicator) and *what construct is scored* (the physical sleep environment, broader sleep-hygiene practices and routines, safe-sleep practices, or the structural characteristics of the dwelling). No single instrument yet captures the "whole family" as a unit; instead, investigators typically assemble a battery of age- and informant-appropriate tools. What follows summarizes the validated measures, what each one is, and the populations in which each was developed and validated.

## Measures of the physical sleep environment

The most directly relevant instrument for the *physical home sleep environment* is the **Assessment of Sleep Environment (ASE)** (Grandner et al., 2022). The ASE is a 13-item self-report scale developed and initially validated in a sample of 1,007 U.S. adults aged 22–60 (the SHADES study). It yields a score reflecting the degree to which the respondent's bedroom environment is disruptive to sleep across domains including light, temperature, noise, safety, comfort, humidity, and smell, and higher scores have been associated with insomnia symptoms and poorer sleep quality. Because it is an adult self-report measure, it is best administered to each adult in a household. For pediatric populations, the analogous instrument is the **Children's and Adolescents' Sleep Environment Scale (CASES)** (Peltz et al., 2022), a 13-item caregiver-report scale validated with 840 U.S. caregivers of children aged 5–18. The CASES scores the child's sleep environment across three empirically derived factors—general environmental hazards, bedding availability, and the presence of electronics—and was validated using exploratory and confirmatory factor analysis in split-half samples.

## Sleep-hygiene and bedtime-routine measures with an environmental component

Several validated sleep-hygiene instruments embed a scored *environmental* subscale, which makes them useful when the environment must be situated within broader behavioral context. The **Sleep Hygiene Practice Scale (SHPS)** (Yang et al., 2010) is a 30-item adult self-report measure (developed in Taiwan among good sleepers and patients with insomnia) that scores maladaptive sleep-hygiene practices across four domains, one of which is a dedicated sleep-environment domain. For adolescents, the **Adolescent Sleep Hygiene Scale–Revised (ASHS-r)** (Storfer-Isser et al., 2013), validated in U.S. urban adolescents aged roughly 16–19, produces a sleep-hygiene score whose confirmatory factor structure includes a distinct sleep-environment subscale alongside physiological, behavioral-arousal, cognitive/emotional, daytime-sleep, and sleep-stability factors. Two instruments from Meltzer's group extend this to younger, self-reporting children: the **Children's Report of Sleep Patterns (CRSP)** (Meltzer et al., 2013), validated in U.S. children aged 8–12, includes a sleep-hygiene module with a scored environmental subscale, allowing the child—rather than only the parent—to rate their sleep environment; and the **Pediatric Sleep Practices Questionnaire (PSPQ)** (Meltzer et al., 2021), validated in U.S. youth aged 8–17, scores five sleep practices, one of which is a room-environment subscale (the others being sleep timing, routines/consistency, technology use, and need for parental presence). Focused specifically on routines, the **Bedtime Routines Questionnaire (BRQ)** (Henderson & Jordan, 2010) is a 31-item caregiver-report measure validated with 226 U.S. caregivers of children aged 2–8 that distinguishes consistent from reactive bedtime routines.

## The family-unit and safe-sleep measures

Only one instrument in this literature scores the *family unit* rather than an individual: the **Family Inventory of Sleep Habits (FISH)** (Malow et al., 2009). Developed in U.S. children aged 4–10 (including children with autism spectrum disorder and typically developing children), the FISH is a parent-reported measure of family sleep hygiene that spans daytime habits, pre-bedtime and bedtime routines, the sleep environment, and—distinctively—parental bedtime behaviors. This combination makes it the natural anchor for any family-level design, because it explicitly incorporates the parent's contribution to the child's sleep context. For the youngest members of a household, the **Parent Newborn Sleep Safety Survey** (Whiteside-Mansell et al., 2017), validated among parents of newborns in high-risk U.S. families, scores caregiver adherence to safe-sleep-environment recommendations (sleep position, surface, bedding, and location) and was validated against direct observation, positioning it as a scored alternative to observational assessment.

## Objective, structural measures of the household

Finally, the literature supports an objective, non-self-report indicator of the household sleeping context: the **Household Crowding Index**, most commonly operationalized as persons-per-room or against a "bedroom standard." While the construct is long-standing, recent peer-reviewed work has compared and validated these objective crowding measures against socioeconomic, health, and well-being outcomes (Torshizian & Grimes, 2021; Cable & Sacker, 2019). Because it is computed rather than perceived, the crowding index is well suited to serve as a low-burden household-level covariate for confounding adjustment and for multilevel models in which individuals are nested within households.

## Synthesis and gaps

Taken together, the literature allows a team to cover the household lifespan with age- and informant-appropriate scored measures—from safe-sleep practices in infancy (Parent Newborn Sleep Safety Survey), through routines and environment in early and middle childhood (BRQ, FISH, CASES, CRSP, PSPQ), to sleep hygiene and environment in adolescence (ASHS-r) and adulthood (ASE, SHPS)—supplemented by an objective structural indicator (Household Crowding Index). Two limitations should be acknowledged in any proposal. First, most of these instruments were developed and validated in U.S. samples (with the SHPS validated in Taiwan), and cross-cultural and Spanish-language validity is not established for several of them; this is a documented gap in the measurement of sleep in Latino families (Blanco, Hyde & Martinez, 2024), and it argues for formal translation and validation work before deployment in Latino populations. Second, and more fundamentally, no single validated instrument scores the *whole family* as a system: the FISH comes closest by incorporating parental behaviors, but a genuine family- or household-level sleep score would most likely need to be constructed by combining a caregiver-report family measure, one or more within-household self-reports, and an objective structural indicator. These two gaps—cultural validation and the absence of a composite family-level metric—represent concrete opportunities that a family/household-level intervention or data-collection effort is well positioned to address.
